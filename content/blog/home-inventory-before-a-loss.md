Make a room-by-room record of your belongings while you can still see them. Start with a slow video, add a written list and supporting records, and test that you can retrieve the backup without entering your home. Waiting until after a fire or burglary turns an ordinary household list into a difficult memory exercise.

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners recommends recording identifying information and purchase details alongside photos. Its current inventory tool supports room or category organization and export. You can use an app, a document or a spreadsheet; the useful part is a record you can actually retrieve. [NAIC inventory guidance](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-understanding-your-homeowners-or-renters-policy), [NAIC home inventory tool](https://content.naic.org/consumer/home-inventory).

## What should a useful inventory contain?

Give each entry an identifier, such as `Kitchen-014`, so its photos and documents stay connected. Use these fields:

| Field | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Location | Room, closet, garage or off-site location |
| Item and quantity | A specific description and an honest count |
| Identification | Brand, model, serial number and distinguishing features |
| Purchase information | Approximate date, seller and original price, if known |
| Evidence | Photo filenames, receipt, order confirmation or appraisal |
| Current comparison | A comparable replacement product and the date you checked it |
| Notes | Condition, gifts, inherited items or uncertainty about age |

Separate a known fact from an estimate. “Bought in 2021, approximately $350” is more useful than a falsely precise purchase date. Original purchase price and today's comparable replacement price belong in different columns. They answer different questions.

Photograph the actual object as well as its label. A retailer's image helps identify a comparison product but does not show that the object was in your home. Save your own images at their original quality, and keep a readable copy of a receipt rather than only a link to an account that might close.

## How do you finish without cataloguing everything in one weekend?

Work in three passes. This is a practical workflow, rather than an insurer's required form.

**First, capture the rooms.** Stand in the doorway, slowly record the walls and furniture, and open cabinets and drawers where it is safe and appropriate. Narrate what you see. Include the closets, attic, garage and storage areas. Holiday decorations and tools are easy to forget; the NAIC specifically flags rarely used belongings. [NAIC homeowners preparation checklist](https://content.naic.org/consumer/homeowners-insurance.htm).

**Second, document the items hardest to reconstruct.** Photograph electronics, appliances, specialized equipment, valuable collections and custom furnishings individually. Capture labels without moving something heavy or unsafe. Search your email and retailer accounts for their receipts. For a gift, save any available documentation and note who gave it to you and when.

**Third, count the ordinary belongings.** Clothing, cookware, linens and books can represent many small purchases. For your preparedness record, use sensible categories with counts and photos: “eight cotton bath towels” is stronger than “bathroom stuff.” If a loss occurs, ask the insurer whether it needs individual entries or will accept categories for the particular claim.

Do not assume a grouping rule applies everywhere. California has specific protections for category groupings in certain contents claims involving a total loss of a primary residence related to a declared state of emergency. Those conditions matter, and they are not a national inventory standard. [California's 2026 disaster claims guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0200-industry/0050-renew-license/0200-requirements/upload/2026-Guide-for-Adjusting-Property-Claims-in-California-After-a-Major-Disaster_Final.pdf).

## What does the inventory tell you about insurance value?

An inventory can reveal a mismatch between what you own and the coverage you bought. It cannot turn an uncovered event into a covered loss or remove a policy limit.

Actual cash value generally reflects replacement cost less depreciation. Replacement cost coverage uses the cost of a comparable replacement without a depreciation deduction, subject to the policy's conditions. Ask specifically how those terms apply to **personal property**, even if you already know the valuation terms for the building. [Washington insurance commissioner's valuation explanation](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/insurance-resources/home-insurance/how-home-insurance-works/learn-how-home-insurance-works).

Consider an illustrative inventory entry for a four-year-old television:

- Original receipt: $900.
- Comparable television at today's price: $650.
- Photos: television in the living room, model label and serial number.
- Condition: working before the loss, with the age identified as approximate.

Neither $900 nor $650 is automatically the settlement. The comparable model could require discussion; depreciation, deductible and limits may apply. Under some replacement cost terms, payment of withheld depreciation follows replacement and proof of spending. Ask about deadlines before purchasing a replacement. Texas's consumer guide describes that two-stage process for personal property replacement cost claims. [Texas home insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb025.html).

The point of the entry is to make the discussion specific. “Television” alone leaves size, quality and features unexplained.

## Which belongings need a separate coverage conversation?

Flag jewelry, antiques, art, collectibles, firearms and other valuables for your agent. California's residential insurance guide explains that certain categories have limited coverage and that separately scheduling items may be appropriate. The applicable amounts and causes of loss come from your own contract. [California residential insurance guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/03-res/res-ins-guide.cfm).

Bring a short question list to the conversation: Is the item covered for the risks I care about? Is there a category or per-item limit? Is an appraisal required? Does an endorsement change valuation or the deductible? Keep the written response with the policy, then check the issued endorsement rather than relying only on the conversation.

## Where should you store the inventory?

Keep a copy outside the home, with the photos and supporting records. Washington's consumer guide recommends secure secondary storage and annual updates. [Washington home insurance consumer guide](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/sites/default/files/documents/homeowner-insurance-guide_0.pdf).

Test access from another device. Make sure the account recovery method is usable if your usual phone is gone. Export a copy from any inventory app and check that the export includes the information you expect. Protect sensitive records with strong account security and avoid putting serial numbers, valuables or your address in a public folder.

Add new purchases as they arrive, remove things you sell, and date each review. A short recurring update is easier than reconstructing years of changes.

If damage happens before the inventory is complete, use the records you have. Follow the site's [first 48 hours after property damage guide](/blog/first-48-hours-after-property-damage/) for safety, documentation and reporting, and keep uncertainty visible when rebuilding the list.
