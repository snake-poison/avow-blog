Replacement cost coverage does not always mean a check for the full repair estimate arrives first. Many claims start with a payment reduced for depreciation and the deductible. To understand the remaining money, ask whether the depreciation is **recoverable**, what you must do to recover it, and when.

This guide concerns U.S. homeowners claims. The figures below are illustrations; state law and the actual policy control the valuation and payment process.

## What do actual cash value and replacement cost mean?

Replacement cost is the cost to repair or replace damaged property with comparable kind and quality without deducting depreciation. Actual cash value, often abbreviated ACV, generally reflects replacement cost reduced for depreciation. This is a common insurance distinction across states, also described by the [NAIC](https://content.naic.org/consumer/homeowners-insurance.htm). For example, Washington's Office of the Insurance Commissioner explains it in terms of current value versus replacement cost; the exact legal valuation method still depends on the state and policy. [Washington home insurance guide](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/insurance-resources/home-insurance/how-home-insurance-works/learn-how-home-insurance-works).

Depreciation reflects factors such as age and condition. It is a valuation adjustment, not the deductible. Also, “replacement cost minus depreciation” is a useful starting explanation rather than a universal legal formula for every loss. California, for example, describes different ACV measures for total structural losses versus partial structural and contents losses. [California residential insurance guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/01-consumers/105-type/95-guides/03-res/res-ins-guide.cfm).

Your declarations page's large coverage limit is a ceiling, not a promise to pay that amount for any damage. Read the loss settlement provision and applicable endorsements to learn how the damaged property is valued.

## Can one policy have different settlement terms for different property?

Yes. Different settlement terms within one policy are a possibility to check in any state. For example, the Texas Department of Insurance explains that a house can have replacement cost coverage while personal property has actual cash value coverage, and that some policies settle roof damage differently. Those are policy examples, not terms required of every Texas policy or policies elsewhere. [TDI home insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb025.html).

Check the dwelling, other structures, personal property and roof provisions separately. Look for a roof payment schedule, an ACV roof endorsement or a restriction on particular materials. Ask your agent to identify the controlling forms, rather than relying on a general description such as “full coverage.”

Make a four-row worksheet with the property category, settlement basis, form number and conditions. You want to know whether your old sofa and your damaged roof follow the same payment rules before using one claim payment to plan both replacements.

## Why can the first check be much smaller than the estimate?

For example, the Texas Department of Insurance describes a two-payment process: an initial payment less depreciation and the deductible, followed by eligible depreciation after repairs or replacement, with receipts potentially required. You may encounter this arrangement in other states, but it is not a nationwide payment rule. Your policy and state law determine whether it applies and what documentation and timing are required. [TDI home insurance guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/pubs/consumer/cb025.html).

Consider this simplified example of a covered replacement cost claim:

| Calculation | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Approved replacement cost | $24,000 |
| Less depreciation | $6,000 |
| Actual cash value before deductible | $18,000 |
| Less deductible | $2,000 |
| Initial net payment | $16,000 |
| Potential later depreciation payment | Up to $6,000 |
| Potential combined insurance payments | $22,000 |

The $6,000 later payment is conditional. This example assumes qualifying repairs costing the approved amount, entirely recoverable depreciation, no special limits, and no other adjustments. It does not establish a depreciation rate for your roof or belongings.

If those assumptions hold, your $2,000 deductible remains your contribution. You also need a plan for the timing gap: the contractor may require payment before the insurer releases all eligible depreciation. Review that schedule before signing a contract.

## What makes depreciation recoverable?

Recoverable depreciation is the withheld portion that may be paid when you satisfy the replacement cost conditions. It is not a bonus or a guaranteed second check. An ACV-only policy, by contrast, can leave the depreciation deduction as part of the final settlement. For example, the Texas Department of Insurance's comparison illustrates how ACV coverage can reduce payment compared with replacement cost. The financial distinction is relevant across states; the applicable settlement terms remain policy- and state-dependent. [TDI replacement cost versus ACV comparison](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/home-insurance-policies-replacement-cost-or-actual-cash-value.html).

Ask the insurer to identify:

- Recoverable and nonrecoverable depreciation separately.
- The repair or replacement condition for each property category.
- Whether partial completion can support a partial additional payment.
- The documents needed and the submission deadline.
- How actual incurred costs and policy limits cap payment.

Do not assume the insurer's estimate is a lump sum you receive regardless of what work is done. If you choose less expensive replacements or omit repairs, ask for the resulting calculation in writing. Do the same before choosing an upgrade, changing materials or doing the work yourself.

## How should you document repair or replacement?

Build the documentation around the insurer's approved scope, with a clear record of changes. Ask for its submission instructions before work starts. A practical file includes the estimate, itemized contractor agreement, change orders, invoices, receipts, completion photographs and a list matching replaced contents to their original inventory entries.

Keep accurate records of actual expenditures. An invoice is useful evidence of a charge, but it may not by itself establish that every policy condition has been met. Ask whether the insurer needs proof of payment, a completion inspection or other evidence.

When you submit the request, name the claim number, list the work completed, identify the documents attached and ask for an updated payment ledger. Save the submission and acknowledgment. If only part of the withheld amount is released, request the remaining amount and reason by line item.

## What if the estimate or depreciation looks wrong?

Compare the estimate with the damaged property's actual age, condition, quantities and repair requirements. A wrong roof installation date or an omitted room measurement can affect the calculation. Send dated evidence such as installation records, maintenance invoices, photographs and itemized contractor findings.

Ask which depreciation method was used and which items were depreciated. Rules about labor depreciation differ. California's 2026 disaster adjusting guide, for example, distinguishes repair labor from intrinsic labor in manufactured goods and says repair labor is not subject to physical depreciation. That California rule should not be presented as a nationwide rule. [California 2026 property claims adjusting guide](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0200-industry/0050-renew-license/0200-requirements/upload/2026-Guide-for-Adjusting-Property-Claims-in-California-After-a-Major-Disaster_Final.pdf).

Keep a scope dispute separate from the request for eligible withheld depreciation. Completing the original scope does not explain away an omitted covered repair. Ask how to submit a supplement when additional damage or necessary work becomes apparent.

## Is there a universal deadline to recover depreciation?

No. Read the policy and check applicable state protections. California Insurance Code section 2051.5 sets replacement cost time protections, including different minimum periods for losses related to a declared state of emergency, and addresses extensions. That is a state-specific framework, not a deadline to apply to every homeowner. [California Insurance Code section 2051.5](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=INS&sectionNum=2051.5.).

Ask the insurer for the controlling deadline and its starting event in writing. Calendar it early. If permitting, contractor availability or another issue delays work, document the reason and request an extension before the stated deadline. Also track any separate proof-of-loss or legal action deadlines; one date should not be assumed to replace another.
