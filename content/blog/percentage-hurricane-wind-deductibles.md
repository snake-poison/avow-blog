A 2 percent deductible can mean thousands of dollars out of pocket before your insurer pays a storm claim. The first task is to find **what that percentage multiplies**. The second is to find **which event activates it**. A familiar $1,000 deductible on your declarations page may not be the deductible for the damage in front of you.

This guide concerns U.S. residential property insurance. Examples below are simplified calculations, not predictions of a particular settlement.

## What is a percentage deductible based on?

Using insured value as the base is common across states, as the NAIC's hurricane deductible overview explains. For example, in Texas, the Department of Insurance also describes percentage deductibles this way. This is a common policy structure, not a calculation rule to assume for every policy. Your policy identifies the applicable coverage or other calculation base. [TDI deductible guide](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/tips/deductibles.html), [NAIC hurricane deductible overview](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/hurricane-deductibles).

For an illustrative dwelling coverage limit of $400,000:

| Deductible | Dollar amount |
| --- | ---: |
| 1 percent | $4,000 |
| 2 percent | $8,000 |
| 5 percent | $20,000 |

If an otherwise covered loss is $30,000 and the applicable deductible is $8,000, the simplified payment is $22,000. A $6,000 covered loss would produce no payment under that deductible. These examples assume no depreciation, special limit, coverage exclusion or other adjustment.

Do not substitute your mortgage balance, purchase price or an online home valuation for the base stated in the policy. Put the declarations page beside the deductible endorsement and record the actual limit used.

## Which deductible applies to a storm?

Three labels need separate attention:

- **Hurricane:** activated by the hurricane definition and trigger applicable to the policy.
- **Named storm:** may apply to a broader class of named weather events.
- **Wind or hail:** may apply to damage from wind or hail outside a named hurricane.

The NAIC emphasizes that the applicable trigger determines whether a hurricane, named storm or windstorm deductible applies. The label alone does not settle the question. Ask for the definition, the event trigger and the duration provision together. [NAIC hurricane deductible overview](https://content.naic.org/insurance-topics/hurricane-deductibles).

Build a small policy worksheet before storm season: deductible name, percentage or dollars, calculation base, trigger, and whether it applies per occurrence or on another basis. Include the form number and page. This makes renewal changes easier to spot and gives you a useful reference when the claim estimate arrives.

If a claim handler says “storm deductible,” ask which one. A precise response should identify the policy provision and show the arithmetic.

## Why do Florida hurricane claims need a separate explanation?

Florida's Department of Financial Services explains that the hurricane deductible period begins when the National Hurricane Center issues a hurricane warning for any part of Florida and ends 72 hours after the last hurricane watch or warning for any part of the state terminates. Local landfall time is not the only relevant timestamp. [Florida hurricane deductible guide](https://www.myfloridacfo.com/division/consumers/consumerprotections/floridashurricanedeductible).

Florida also uses a calendar-year framework for covered personal residential hurricane losses when the policyholder remains with the same insurer or insurer group. A later hurricane's deductible is generally the greater of the unused hurricane deductible balance or the all other perils deductible. Separate insured structures and a switch to an unrelated insurer can affect the result. [Florida hurricane deductible guide](https://www.myfloridacfo.com/division/consumers/consumerprotections/floridashurricanedeductible).

That is why the slogan “one deductible per year” needs qualification. It does not promise that every later hurricane claim has a zero deductible. Keep earlier claim determinations and ask the insurer to show the remaining balance. Florida's guidance recommends reporting covered hurricane losses even below the deductible so the insurer can record the credit toward that year's hurricane deductible. [Florida hurricane deductible guide](https://www.myfloridacfo.com/division/consumers/consumerprotections/floridashurricanedeductible).

This calendar-year framework and statewide warning period are Florida-specific rules. Hurricane deductible triggers and how multiple storms are treated vary by state and policy; check the rules where your property is located.

## Does a hurricane deductible mean flood damage is covered?

No. A deductible tells you how a covered loss is shared; it does not create coverage for an excluded cause of damage. FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program explains that most homeowners policies do not cover flood damage. Wind and flood damage from the same storm can therefore involve different policies and claims. [NFIP flood insurance overview](https://www.floodsmart.gov/).

Photograph roof openings, water entry points and water lines when it is safe. Describe what you observed without guessing at the cause. If you carry separate flood and wind policies, keep their declarations and claim numbers in the same folder, clearly identified. Do not assume that an answer from one insurer resolves the other policy's obligations.

## What should you check on the claim estimate?

Start with the covered damage amount, then trace each deduction separately. Depreciation and the deductible do different jobs. An estimate can show both; the first check may therefore be smaller than the covered replacement cost minus the deductible.

Ask the insurer in writing:

1. Which deductible form applies to this loss, and what triggered it?
2. Which coverage limit or other base did you multiply by the percentage?
3. What dollar amount did that calculation produce?
4. Is the deductible applied per occurrence, per structure or under a calendar-year provision?
5. If there were earlier covered storms, how were those losses credited?

Send the relevant declarations page if your calculation differs. A clear discrepancy, such as using $450,000 when the applicable limit was $400,000, is easier to investigate than a general complaint about the check being too small.

## Can a contractor make the deductible disappear?

Be careful with a proposal that depends on inflated paperwork or a secret rebate. For a state-specific example, the Texas Department of Insurance explains that Texas contractors cannot waive, rebate or absorb a property insurance deductible and that an insurer can request proof of payment. Do not assume Texas's prohibition or proof requirements are identical elsewhere; check your state's rules. [TDI roofing and insurance rules](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/consumer/storms/roofing-and-insurance-know-the-law.html).

Compare written scopes and prices, and record what you actually pay. Do not sign an invoice stating a price you did not incur. If the deductible is unaffordable, discuss a documented payment arrangement with the contractor and ask the insurer what evidence it needs before agreeing to the work.

## What should you do at renewal?

Calculate the deductible in dollars again whenever the dwelling limit changes. An unchanged percentage can produce a larger dollar obligation after a coverage increase. Compare that obligation with money you could realistically access following a storm, alongside the premium and coverage terms. Ask your agent to explain the available choices using your actual policy limits.

A useful preparedness number is the dollar deductible for each relevant peril. Write it down while there is still time to plan for it.
