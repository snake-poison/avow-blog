    answer: The first notice of loss, or FNOL, is the policyholder's first report of a loss to their insurer, by phone, app, email or through their agent. It opens the claim and starts the insurer's handling deadlines.

The first notice of loss is the shortest conversation on a property claim and the one every later step depends on. It is usually taken in a hurry, by someone who will not handle the claim, from a policyholder who is upset and standing in a damaged house. What gets written down in those few minutes is what the field adjuster reads first, what a reviewer checks later, and what an appraiser or a court may read a year from now.

A complete first notice is not a long one. It gets the facts the claim will need, in the policyholder's words, and leaves cause, coverage and value for later.

## Why first notice decides how a claim goes

Most of the friction on a property claim is not about coverage. It is about facts nobody captured at the start: a date of loss that turns out to be a date of discovery, a second structure nobody mentioned, a mortgage company left off the payment, a family living in a hotel for a week before anyone knew. Each one costs a call, a reinspection or a supplement, and each delay is time the policyholder spends wondering whether the claim is being handled at all.

::post-pullquote
Every gap at first notice is paid for later, in cycle time, reinspections or a complaint.
::

The same applies whoever takes the notice: a carrier's intake team, an independent adjusting firm, or an agent's office passing the loss on. The checklist below works for all of them, and for a public adjuster opening a file on the same loss.

## What to capture, in order

::post-steps
:post-when[First]

## Confirm the policy and the people

- **Policy number and named insureds**, exactly as on the declarations page.
- **The best contact**, how they want to be reached, and where they are staying now.
- **Anyone else authorised to speak for them:** a spouse, a property manager, a public adjuster or an attorney. Ask for the letter of representation if there is one.
- **Mortgagee or lienholder**, so the payment is right the first time.
- **Any contract already signed** with a restoration company or contractor, including an assignment of benefits where state law allows them.

:post-when[Second]

## Get two dates, not one

Ask when the loss happened and, separately, when they found it. For a storm or a fire they are the same day. For water, mould or a slow roof leak they often are not, and the gap matters: it bears on exclusions for repeated seepage or wear, on whether notice was prompt, and on which policy term applies.

If the policyholder does not know when it started, write that down. "Unknown, discovered 10/03 when the ceiling stained" is a fact. A guessed date becomes a fact nobody meant to give.

:post-when[Third]

## Record what they saw, in their words

Ask open questions: *What did you notice? Where? What did you do then?* Write the answer down close to how it was said, and mark it as the policyholder's account.

Avoid questions that lead toward a cause or an exclusion, and do not fill in a cause they did not give. The field adjuster will determine cause from the inspection. The intake notes should give them the policyholder's observations to test, not a conclusion to defend.

::post-callout{tone="warn" title="Observations, not conclusions"}
"Has it been leaking for a while?" invites a guess that can read like an admission of long-term seepage. "When did you first see water?" gets a date. Ask the second kind.
::

:post-when[Fourth]

## Map the scope of the damage

- **Which rooms and areas**, and which floors.
- **Other structures:** a detached garage, a fence, a shed.
- **Contents**, in general terms. A full inventory comes later.
- **Whether the home is livable.** If it is not, open the loss-of-use or additional living expenses conversation now, before the policyholder spends money they do not know is covered.

:post-when[Fifth]

## Ask what has been done to stop it

Policies require the insured to protect the property from further damage. Ask what they have done (water shut off, roof tarped, openings boarded, drying started) and who is on site. Then tell them plainly:

- Reasonable temporary repairs are part of the claim. **Keep every receipt.**
- **Hold off on permanent repairs** until the adjuster has seen the damage or agreed otherwise.
- **Keep damaged items**, or a sample of carpet and drywall, until they are documented.

A policyholder who hears this on the first call does not throw out the evidence the adjuster needed on the third.

:post-when[Sixth]

## Find out what documentation already exists

Ask whether they have photos or video from before cleanup, and how to get them: an upload link, an email address, the app. Ask them to keep taking photos as mitigation goes on. Note anything else in hand: a plumber's invoice naming the failed part, a fire department report, a mitigation company's moisture readings.

:post-when[Last]

## Set the next steps in writing

Before the call ends, the policyholder should know the claim number, who will contact them, when, and what they should do in the meantime. Send it in writing the same day. A short email that repeats the call, with the claim number, the adjuster's name and the first three things to do, prevents most of the "nobody has called me" complaints that follow a catastrophe.
::

## What each detail protects later

| Captured at first notice          | What it prevents later                                         |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Date of loss and date discovered  | Disputes over late notice, seepage exclusions and policy term  |
| Policyholder's own account        | A cause in the file that nobody actually stated                |
| Mortgagee and other payees        | Reissued payments and delayed repairs                          |
| Representation and contracts      | Communications going to the wrong party                        |
| Habitability                      | Unplanned living expenses and an upset family                  |
| Mitigation done and advised       | Arguments over spread damage and unpaid emergency costs        |
| Existing photos and reports       | Reinspections to see damage that has since been cleaned up     |

## Questions that cost a claim later

- **"Is this covered?"** answered on the spot, either way. A yes creates an expectation the policy may not support; a no can be a misrepresentation if the adjuster later finds coverage. The honest answer is that the adjuster will decide from the full policy and the inspection, and when.
- **"How much do you think it will cost?"** A number given in shock on the first call tends to anchor the whole claim, high or low.
- **"What caused it?"** asked as if it needs an answer today. Many policyholders will guess to be helpful.
- **Leaving out the deductible.** Policyholders who learn about a percentage wind or hail deductible from the first payment letter feel misled, even when nothing was hidden. Say what the deductible is and how it applies.

## When a public adjuster is already involved

A growing share of property claims arrive with representation from the start. Treat the letter of representation as part of first notice: confirm the public adjuster's licence in the state where the property is, record which communications go through them, and keep sending the policyholder anything the policy or state law says must go to the insured.

For public adjusters, the same checklist is the start of your own file. The carrier's intake notes are often the first thing you ask for, and gaps in them are easier to correct in week one than in appraisal.

## Where policyholders are coming from

The policyholder on the other end of the call has usually done some of this already, or none of it. Our [guide for homeowners on the first 48 hours after property damage](/blog/first-48-hours-after-property-damage/) covers the same ground from their side: make it safe, stop the damage, document before cleaning, report, and start a claim file. A policyholder who has read it will arrive with photos, receipts and a call log. One who has not will need the fifth and sixth steps above spelled out.

::post-checklist{title="First notice of loss checklist"}
- [ ] Policy number, named insureds and best contact confirmed
- [ ] Representatives, assignments and contracts recorded, with letters requested
- [ ] Mortgagee or lienholder recorded
- [ ] Date of loss and date discovered, recorded separately
- [ ] What happened, in the policyholder's words, marked as their account
- [ ] Rooms, structures and contents affected
- [ ] Habitability checked; living expenses explained if needed
- [ ] Mitigation done so far, and temporary-repair and receipt guidance given
- [ ] Existing photos, video and reports requested
- [ ] Deductible explained
- [ ] Claim number, next contact and timeline sent in writing
::

*This is general information, not legal advice. Policy wording, claim-handling regulations and the rules on public adjusters and assignments of benefits differ from state to state.*
